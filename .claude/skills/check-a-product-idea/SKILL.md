# Check a product idea

A study of an idea answers one question: should Louis spend months on this? It is research, never a build. The
answer is a report he can read in ten minutes, with a verdict and a next step. Six stops come first.

1. **Nothing is guessed.** Louis, 2026-11-23: "do not guess anything". A price, a market's size, a count of
   businesses and a law are read at their source that day and linked. What could not be read is said to be not
   known, and listed at the end. A judgement of ours is called a judgement.
2. **Start from AI, never from the rival's product.** Louis, 2026-11-27, of a first study that added AI to a
   copy of the rival: "start it with AI first thinking not how can I get AI into it. AI is already in and
   everything is AI driven". The product is designed as an agent first (*The AI-first design*), and only then
   held against the rivals.
3. **No code, no plan, no TODO row.** A study changes nothing in the build order. A verdict of "build it" still
   only proposes, and the plan is `/plan-a-feature`'s on his word.
4. **A compliment is not demand.** "Sounds great" from a buyer counts for nothing. Only what they did in the
   past, and what they commit now, counts (*The buyer talks*).
5. **One verdict, and it may be no.** The study says yes, no, or not yet, in its first sentence, with what
   would change it. A no that is well sourced is a good result.
6. **Read what we have first.** Project memory for an earlier study of the same idea, then `origin/main` after
   `git fetch origin main`, never the clone's own `main`.

## The steps

1. **Read memory and the repo.** An earlier study is continued, not run again. Read `TODO.md`, `ROADMAP.md`
   and `src/docs/GOAL.md` on `origin/main`, for what the framework has, what is planned and what Louis is
   learning.
2. **Say the idea as a hypothesis**, in Kevin Hale's three parts: the problem (who has it, and how often), the
   solution (what we would build), and the insight (why it would work, and why us). Say which reading of his
   ask you took. An idea with no problem named is a solution in search of a problem, and the study says so.
3. **Draw the AI-first design** before reading any rival's features: who talks to the agent and where, what
   the agent does on its own, what a person still decides, and what wakes it. One picture and one worked day.
4. **Read the rivals**, at their own pages that day: two to four direct rivals (NN/g's number), one South
   African one if there is one, and whoever already sells the AI-first version. For each: the plans, the prices
   as shown with their currency, what sits behind each tier, and whether a free plan exists. A page that hides
   its price is said to hide it.
5. **Size the market**, from reports whose publisher is named. Two or three that disagree are shown side by
   side, never averaged. A paid report's teaser is called weak. Look for the South African figure, and say
   plainly when there is none.
6. **Hold the product against the framework.** One row per thing the product needs: what the code has today
   (name the project or the file), what the TODO plans (its id), and the gap. Check the code, never memory: a
   `grep` for the part settles it.
7. **Work the money through**, against Louis's goal (2026-11-23: "the goal is 60k a month after tax"): how many
   paying customers at which price. A price we would choose is called ours, not measured.
8. **Run the pre-mortem.** Suppose it was built and failed a year on, and write the three likeliest reasons.
   Each is a risk in the report, with what would show it early.
9. **Give three ways to play it**, each with what is built, what speaks for it and what against, and the one
   recommended to test (rule 10 in `src/docs/CLAUDE.md`).
10. **Write the verdict and the next step.** The next step is the cheapest thing that could prove the idea
    wrong, almost always five talks with buyers (*The buyer talks*). Say where it sits in the TODO as it is
    (the demand check, F62), and which GOAL.md skills the build would teach.
11. **When Louis brings back what buyers said**, hold it against the pass mark written in the study, and say
    whether the verdict moves.

## The AI-first design

A rival built before 2023 started with forms and added AI on top. We start the other way, and that is the only
edge one developer has over a company of hundreds. Answer these four in the report.

| Question | A good answer looks like |
|---|---|
| Where do people talk to it? | A channel they already use (WhatsApp, a chat on a site, voice), not a new app with forms |
| What does the agent do on its own? | The low-risk work: logging, reminding, answering, drafting |
| What does a person still decide? | Anything with money, safety or a promise in it. The line is a setting, as F41 and F7 plan |
| What wakes it with nobody asking? | A clock or an event: something due, something late, a weekly summary |

Then read the framework table again with this design, since it usually drops the screens and the phone app
from the first build. A part the design leans on that we do not have, such as a channel, gets its own cost and
terms read before any plan.

## What makes an idea good

From Kevin Hale's talk for Y Combinator. A good problem has at least one of these, and the report says which,
with its evidence: popular, growing, urgent, expensive, mandatory (a law asks for it), frequent. The insight is
an unfair advantage, and he names five: the founder (a rare expert), the market (growing about 20% a year), the
product ("10X better than the competition"), acquisition (growth that costs no money), and monopoly (it gets
harder to beat as it grows). At least one, said honestly. "None found" is a finding.

For Louis, add two of his own. Does it prove the framework (2026-11-23: "we need proof the framework works")?
And does building it teach a skill in GOAL.md's map that he has not yet learned?

## The buyer talks

From Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*. Its three rules: don't pitch the idea, ask specific questions about the
past, and listen more than you talk. So the study hands Louis exact questions, since he has no sales
background: how do you keep track of this today, what happened the last time it went wrong, what did that
cost, what have you tried or paid for. The price question comes last, and a yes to it counts only with a
commitment: a deposit, a pilot date, an introduction to someone else.

The pass mark is written in the study before the talks, such as three of five naming the problem unprompted
and one committing. Claude drafts the questions. Louis makes the calls.

## What the report holds

A Claude Doc, in this order: the short answer (the verdict in three paragraphs), what the product has to do,
the AI-first design with its picture, the rivals and their prices in one table, the market, the framework
table, three ways to play it, the verdict and next step, the sources, and what was not read.

## Checks

- The first sentence is the verdict, and a reader who stops there knows what to do.
- Every number has a link beside it or the words "not known" or "ours".
- The design would still make sense with every rival's name removed.
- The framework table names code that exists on `origin/main` today.
- Nothing in the repo changed.

## Handing it over

The reply gives the verdict, the two or three reasons, the next step and the report's link. Save the verdict,
the prices read, the pass mark and what was not read to project memory, so a later thread continues from them.

## An example

The CMMS study of 2026-11-27, after Louis asked to "investigate the plausibility" of a system like MaintainX.
The first pass read four rivals' pricing pages (MaintainX and Fiix both free at the bottom, paid seats from $20
to $45 a user a month), found three market reports near $1.5 billion that disagreed on the details, and held
the product against the framework: tenants, a scheduler, notifications and an offline phone app were missing.
Its verdict was no, because it had designed a copy of MaintainX with AI added. Louis sent it back with stop
2's words. Designed AI-first, with everyone talking to one agent on WhatsApp and no forms, most of the missing
half fell away, and the verdict became "plausible, not proven", with five buyer talks as the next step. The
South African market's size, the count of small businesses and the Driven Machinery Regulations could not be
read, and the report listed all three.
